Week 9, Day 4

enumerate and zip

Two small built-ins remove a lot of clutter. enumerate gives you each item together with its position, so you never need range(len(...)) again. zip walks through several sequences side by side, pairing their items up. Today you’ll use both, learn how they work underneath, and use zip to turn grids round in one line.

By the end of today you can

  • Loop over items and their positions with enumerate, starting from any number
  • Walk through two or more sequences in step with zip, and know what happens when their lengths differ
  • Compare neighbours with zip(items, items[1:])
  • Transpose a grid and unzip pairs with zip(*...)
  • Explain that enumerate and zip produce their pairs one at a time, and can only be used once

When the lengths differ

zip stops as soon as the shortest sequence runs out, and says nothing about it:

That’s handy sometimes, and a silent bug at others, when you expected the lengths to match. In Python 3.10 and later you can write zip(a, b, strict=True) to make it raise an error instead. Otherwise, check the lengths yourself when they matter.

Careful

If two lists should line up but might not, zip hides the problem by stopping early. Check len(a) == len(b) first, or use strict=True.

Lazy, and used up

Look at what enumerate and zip give you on their own:

They don’t make a list of all the pairs at once. They’re iterators: objects that produce the next pair only when a loop (or list) asks for it. That saves memory when the sequences are long. But an iterator can be used only once: after you’ve gone through it, it’s empty. The second list(z) gives [].

Common mistake

Don’t store a zip or enumerate and loop over it twice. If you need the pairs again, turn them into a list first (pairs = list(zip(a, b))) or call zip again.
