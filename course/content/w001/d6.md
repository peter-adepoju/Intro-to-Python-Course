## Asking the user: input()

`input()` pauses the program, waits for the user to type something and press Enter, and gives back what they typed. Put a question (a **prompt**) in the brackets so the user knows what to type.

:::example title="Run me and answer"
name = input("What's your name? ")
print("Hello,", name)
:::

When you press **Run**, the console waits for you: type your answer at the cursor and press Enter. Now watch what happens in slow motion. This is the most important line pattern of the week:

:::widget inputflow caption="age = int(input('Age? ')) step by step. Try typing 17, then 'seventeen', then 17.5."
{}
:::

### input() always gives a string

Whatever the user types, even `42`, `input()` gives back a **string**. To do arithmetic, convert it:

:::example error=yes
age = input("How old are you? ")
print("Next year you'll be", age + 1)
:::

That crashes with a `TypeError` (you can't add a string and an int). Convert with `int()` or `float()`:

:::example title="Fixed" try=17
age = int(input("How old are you? "))
print("Next year you'll be", age + 1)
:::

Python works from the inside out: `input(...)` runs first and gives a string; `int(...)` turns it into a number; then `age` points at that number.

:::tip
Leave a space at the end of your prompt, `"Name? "`, so the user's typing doesn't stick to the question mark.
:::

What if the user types something that isn't a number? `int("hello")` raises a `ValueError`. For now, that's fine. In Week 3 you'll learn to ask again, and in Week 11 to catch the error.

## print() in depth

You've used `print()` with several items separated by commas. Two extra settings, **`sep`** and **`end`**, control what goes *between* items and what goes *at the end*:

- `sep` is put between items. The default is one space, `" "`.
- `end` is put after the last item. The default is a new line, `"\n"`, which is why each `print()` starts a new line.

:::widget printlab caption="Set sep and end and see every character print() writes. Spaces show as dots and new lines as ↵."
{"values": "'Ada', 36, 'London'"}
:::

:::predict
print("2026", "10", "26", sep="-")
print("Loading", end="")
print("...", end="")
print("done")
:::

## f-strings

An **f-string** is a string with an `f` before the quote. Inside it, anything in curly brackets `{ }` is worked out as Python and inserted into the text.

:::example
name = "Ada"
age = 36
print(f"{name} is {age} years old.")
print(f"In 10 years she'll be {age + 10}.")
:::

Compare with what you've done before:

```python
print(name, "is", age, "years old.")          # commas: spaces added for you
print(name + " is " + str(age) + " years old.")  # + : you manage spaces and str()
print(f"{name} is {age} years old.")           # f-string: clearest of all
```

f-strings are what professional Python programmers use almost all the time. Inside the brackets you can put any expression:

:::predict
price = 1250
qty = 3
print(f"{qty} x {price} = {qty * price}")
print(f"{'shout'.upper() if False else 'quiet'}")
:::

(Don't worry about that last line's details: it shows that even complicated expressions work inside `{ }`. You'll understand every part of it by Week 2.)

### Formatting numbers

After a colon inside the brackets, you can add a **format spec** saying exactly how to show the value. Play with all the settings:

:::widget fstringlab caption="Build a format spec and see Python's exact output. Yellow underlines are padding. Try width 12 with each alignment, and a comma for thousands."
{"value": "1234567.891", "name": "total"}
:::

The ones you'll use most:

| f-string | Result | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| `f"{3.14159:.2f}"` | `3.14` | 2 decimal places |
| `f"{1234567:,}"` | `1,234,567` | thousands separators |
| `f"{0.256:.1%}"` | `25.6%` | as a percentage |
| `f"{'hi':>6}"` | `    hi` | right-align in 6 characters |
| `f"{'hi':<6}"` | `hi    ` | left-align in 6 |
| `f"{'hi':^6}"` | `  hi  ` | centre in 6 |
| `f"{7:03}"` | `007` | pad with zeros to width 3 |

Alignment makes tables:

:::example title="A receipt"
item1, price1 = "Bread", 1200
item2, price2 = "Milk", 950.5
item3, price3 = "Eggs (crate)", 4500
print(f"{'Item':<15}{'Price':>10}")
print("-" * 25)
print(f"{item1:<15}{price1:>10,.2f}")
print(f"{item2:<15}{price2:>10,.2f}")
print(f"{item3:<15}{price3:>10,.2f}")
print("-" * 25)
print(f"{'Total':<15}{price1 + price2 + price3:>10,.2f}")
:::

### A debugging trick

Put `=` after an expression in an f-string, and Python prints the expression *and* its value. It's the quickest way to check what your variables hold:

:::example
width = 7
height = 4
print(f"{width=} {height=} {width * height=}")
:::

## Input → process → output

Almost every program follows the same shape: **get input**, **process** it, **show output**. Here it is in a small but complete program:

:::example title="Tip calculator" try=12000,10
bill = float(input("Bill amount: "))
percent = int(input("Tip percentage: "))
tip = bill * percent / 100
total = bill + tip
print(f"Tip: {tip:,.2f}")
print(f"Total: {total:,.2f}")
:::

Run it and X-ray it. When you write your own programs, plan them in this shape first: *what do I need to ask, what do I calculate, what do I show?*

## Forge: the weekly challenge

Every sixth day is a **Forge**: the day you combine the whole week's skills into one bigger program, test yourself on everything, and review. Give yourself about 40 minutes. Plan first (what's the input, what's the processing, what's the output?), then build it one piece at a time, running as you go.

:::exercise id=forge points=40 title="Forge: the receipt printer"
Write a program for a small shop. It asks for an item name, its unit price (which may have decimals) and the quantity (a whole number). Then it prints a receipt like the one below, where:

- the first and last lines are 30 `=` characters;
- `Item`, `Unit price`, `Quantity` and `Subtotal` are left-aligned in 15 characters, and the values are right-aligned in 15;
- money has thousands separators and 2 decimal places;
- VAT is 7.5% of the subtotal;
- the total is subtotal plus VAT.

With the inputs `Rice (5kg)`, `8500` and `3`, the console shows:

```
Item: Rice (5kg)
Unit price: 8500
Quantity: 3
==============================
Item                Rice (5kg)
Unit price            8,500.00
Quantity                     3
Subtotal             25,500.00
VAT (7.5%)            1,912.50
TOTAL                27,412.50
==============================
```
@@starter
item = input("Item: ")

@@tests
def test_rice():
    "rice example"
    expect_output("""Item: Rice (5kg)
Unit price: 8500

Item                Rice (5kg)

Subtotal             25,500.00
VAT (7.5%)            1,912.50

    "a second order"
    expect_output("""Item: Pen
Unit price: 150.5

Unit price              150.50
Quantity                    12

VAT (7.5%)              135.45
TOTAL                 1,941.45
==============================""", inputs=["Pen", "150.5", "12"], echo=True)
@@hint
Read the price with `float(input(...))` and the quantity with `int(input(...))`. Each receipt line is `f"{label:<15}{value:>15,.2f}"`, except the item name (no number format) and the quantity (no decimals).

print(f"{'Subtotal':<15}{subtotal:>15,.2f}")

## Weekly quiz

This quiz covers the whole week. Questions you miss come back in your **daily review** on the days ahead, spaced out so they stick.

:::quiz id=weekly
- q: What does the processor's program counter hold?
  options: ["The number of programs running", "The address of the next instruction", "The result of the last sum", "The time"]
  answer: 1
  explain: "It points at the next instruction to fetch, like the yellow pointer in the X-ray."
- q: What does this print?
  type: output
  code: |
    print(type(10 / 2))
  answer: "<class 'float'>"
  explain: "/ always gives a float."
- q: What does this print?
  type: output
  code: |
    a = 3
    b = a
    a += 4
    print(a, b)
  answer: "7 3"
  explain: "a += 4 makes a point at a new object, 7. b still points at 3."
- q: What does this print?
  type: output
  code: |
    print(2 + 3 * 2 ** 2)
  answer: "14"
  explain: "Power first (4), then multiply (12), then add."
- q: What does `-11 // 3` give?
  type: text
  answer: "-4"
  explain: "-3.67 rounded down is -4."
- q: What does this print?
  type: output

    s = "Forge"

  explain: "s[1:3] is 'or'; s[-1] is 'e'."
